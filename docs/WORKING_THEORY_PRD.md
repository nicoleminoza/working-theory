# Working Theory PRD

## Purpose

Working Theory is an interactive application artifact for Nicole Miñoza’s Product Marketing leadership application.

It is not a conventional portfolio and not a collection of project summaries.

Its purpose is to make Nicole’s judgment visible.

After spending time with Working Theory, a reader should believe that Nicole can walk into a complicated business, understand what matters, make a sharp call, bring Product and GTM together, and build the system around it.

The experience should support this positioning:

> **Product Marketing leader with unusually strong Product judgment.**

The unit is the judgment, not the project.

The governing editorial test is:

> **Can I watch the business judgment happen?**

## Audience

Primary audience:
- Product Marketing hiring manager
- Product leadership
- Marketing leadership
- interviewers evaluating senior Product Marketing judgment

The experience should reward a reader who cares about positioning and narrative, commercial judgment, technical fluency, customer understanding, launch and rollout strategy, product influence, organizational adoption, evidence and measurement, and AI-native ways of working.

## Role signals this experience should support

Working Theory should provide credible evidence that Nicole can:
- own positioning and narrative across complex audiences
- lead launches and transitions end to end
- shape what gets built and when
- operate as a strategic peer to Product and Sales
- understand customer needs deeply enough to challenge internal assumptions
- work across technical, business, and creative constraints
- reason with data and revise a point of view when evidence changes
- build systems and repeatable ways of working
- use AI across research, synthesis, design, prototyping, and implementation

Working Theory does **not** need to prove every line of a job description. Literal gaps such as CRM ownership, formal competitive intelligence, deal desk operations, and conventional SaaS pricing/packaging can be covered by the résumé, application note, or interview.

Do not turn the cases into a competency matrix.

## Working Theory concept

Working Theory means:

> **A point of view strong enough to act on, and open enough to change when the evidence does.**

The hidden reasoning grammar is:

> evidence → tension → judgment → reality pushes back → model changes → action → proof → earned learning

Do not expose that as a visible framework.

# Problem 01 — Shared Text Engine

## Role in the set

Problem 01 proves senior strategic influence and organizational adoption.

It proves platform strategy, technical judgment, executive influence, team-level adoption, sequencing, and governance.

Core question:

> **How do you get a strategic platform adopted when you do not control the teams that must choose it?**

## Headline

> **The company needed one platform. Every team had a reason to say no.**

## Cold-reader context

Adobe’s text engine is the layout and shaping system that turns text into the typography visible in a document.

The complexity matters especially in CJK, right-to-left scripts, bidirectional scripts such as Arabic and Hebrew, and interoperability across Adobe applications.

Fragmented text systems created reflow, inconsistent behavior, duplicated complex-script fixes, and duplicated infrastructure.

## Express decision

Adobe Express needed professional text on the web.

The easier local option was browser-native HTML/CSS.

The harder option was bringing Adobe’s shared text engine to the web.

CSS was cheaper in the short term but would have created another source of truth, interoperability risk, reflow risk, duplicate infrastructure, and reduced access to deeper Adobe typography capabilities.

Canonical judgment:

> **I pushed for the shared engine.**

## Ownership

> I set the product direction and made the business case for one shared engine. Engineering owned the architecture, the implementation, the migration mechanics, and the performance work. I owned getting executives on board, the adoption strategy, the sequencing, the success criteria, and cross-product governance.

## Company-level approval

Leadership backed and funded the strategy in 2020.

But that did not make migration a local priority inside other product teams.

Core realization:

> **I had solved the company-level argument. I had not solved the team-level one.**

Supporting line:

> **Permission is not priority.**

## Adoption strategy

Nicole stopped treating adoption as one persuasion problem.

Each team was making a different product decision.

### Express

Interoperability with Photoshop and a path toward shared text behavior on web.

### Illustrator

CJK, Indic, and complex-script capability.

### Photoshop

Performance evidence, lower migration risk, and proof from earlier adopters.

## Sequence

Approved sequence:
- Express — 2021
- Illustrator — 2023
- Photoshop — 2024

Supporting learning:

> **Earlier adopters reduced uncertainty for later teams.**

The first migrations also exposed version skew, release-cadence pressure, and regression risk.

## Governance

Canonical turn:

> **The problem changed again.**

Once multiple teams depended on one shared engine, independent release decisions could create version skew, compatibility problems, and conflicting roadmap priorities.

Nicole started the Type Working Group and ran it for two years.

It became the standing cross-product mechanism for resolving the shared implications of engine changes.

Canonical close:

> **The work moved from migration to governance.**

## Proof

Approved proof:
- **12 of 12 targeted apps migrated**
- Photoshop included
- InDesign intentionally not targeted because the shared engine was not the right fit
- ~15M monthly users on the shared engine
- ~40× faster core typographic actions on the web
- 200+ complex-script text-layout issues resolved

## Final theory

> **A company-level strategy doesn’t become a team-level priority on its own.**

Supporting line:

> **Executive backing creates permission. Adoption happens when the work solves a problem the team already owns.**

# Problem 02 — Usage-based partner economics

## Role in the set

Problem 02 is the differentiator.

It proves commercial judgment, business-model reasoning, technical fluency in service of the business, Product implications, market translation, partner economics, customer value, and staged migration.

Core question:

> **How do you make a difficult business-model change work across Product and market?**

It should not read primarily as a telemetry case, product architecture case, deactivation-history case, or generic cross-functional leadership case.

## Headline

> **The product was growing. The economics were breaking.**

## Situation

After the 2018 access change, activation limits were gone, Adobe Fonts was included across Creative Cloud plans, font activations rose quickly, royalties rose with them, and foundries were still being paid based on activation.

By summer 2019, the royalty budget was about $20M, with a steep trajectory.

Core diagnosis:

> **Activation had stopped meaning use.**

With no limits, customers had little reason to turn fonts off. Adobe was paying for fonts that were switched on whether or not they were actually being used.

Canonical reframing:

> **The launch hadn’t failed. It had worked, on an economic model that no longer fit the product.**

## Options

### 1. Renegotiate rates, partner by partner

Lower cost, but hundreds of separate negotiations and royalties still tied to activation.

### 2. Limit the library or customer access

Lower royalty exposure, but at the cost of customer value and the 2018 access promise.

### 3. Pay for use instead of activation

Fixes the root cause, but changes how every foundry gets paid, depends on product tracking that was not yet broadly available, and creates customer risk around deactivation/uninstall behavior.

Do not make the winning option look inevitable in hindsight.

## Ownership

> My GM made the call to pay for use instead of activation.

> **As head of product and product marketing, I led what that required across product, partners, and customers.**

Do not imply Nicole personally negotiated every foundry contract, owned Legal, built telemetry, or implemented app changes.

## Product implication

Canonical headline:

> **Payment now depended on something the product couldn’t yet see.**

Approved definition of use:

> Opened a document with the font and made an edit, or picked it from the font menu.

Canonical ownership boundary:

> I specified the minimum behavior every app had to support. Engineering and the app teams built it.

Do not let telemetry become the hero.

## Staged transition

The chronology should prove sequencing, not teach the full history of Adobe Fonts deactivation.

### 2019

Before automation was ready, customers were asked to manually turn off fonts they were not using.

### 2020

Manual uninstall and reinstall became a clearer customer-management behavior, framed around a shorter font list and better performance.

### 2021

Automatic inactivity-based uninstalling began for users still operating under the activation model as tracking and product support came online.

### 2022

Most customers were now on the usage model. Automatic uninstalling remained only for those still on the activation model.

## Customer value

Canonical PMM turn:

> **The business reason was economic. The customer reason had to be useful on its own.**

Approved customer-facing message:

> **Uninstall fonts that are no longer in use to keep your font list short and improve performance. Reinstall them whenever you need them again.**

Do not say:

> Nothing is being taken away.

Canonical transition:

> **The message helped. The rollout had to make it true.**

## Rollout safeguards

### Window

> I pushed for a long window before an unused font was automatically uninstalled. We started at nine months, brought it to six, and settled at four to six.

### Recoverability

> Any font could be reinstalled when it was needed again. That is what made the customer message true.

### Enterprise

> Enterprise customers stayed on the old terms through the first year, so the highest-risk accounts weren’t forced through the change first.

Do not restore standalone rows for In the product or Sales and Support.

## Audience translation

### Foundries

> The activation spike after the limits were removed wasn’t a sustainable baseline. The new model would bring growth back to a steadier path, through a staged transition rather than a cliff.

### App teams

> The ask stayed deliberately narrow: log use, expire, deactivate, reactivate. Enough to support the new model without turning it into a larger product rebuild.

Do not add a duplicate Enterprise audience row.

## Proof

Approved proof:
- **$1M saved in the first two quarters**
- against a royalty budget of about $20M with steep growth projected
- **0 foundry partners lost**
- qualitative observation: there was very little discussion of the deactivations across support channels, forums, and social media

Do not add superseded metrics such as usage +12%, cost per user -10%, or royalty growth ~10%.

## Final theory

> **A business model only works if the product can measure what it values.**

Supporting line:

> **Changing the economics meant changing what the product could observe. The business model depended on product capability.**

Do not restore:

> You can't pay for usage you can't see.

# Problem 03 — Typekit → Adobe Fonts

## Role in the set

Problem 03 is the clearest Product Marketing case.

It proves positioning judgment, narrative, GTM leadership, audience-specific proof, partner handling, product implications of positioning, and measurement against the promise.

Core question:

> **What should the product mean?**

Do not let Problem 03 become a generic rebrand story.

## Headline

> **The product was no longer something customers bought.**

## Core situation

Typekit had been a standalone font subscription service. In 2018, activation limits were removed, the library became broadly available inside Creative Cloud, and the standalone purchase decision mostly disappeared.

The GM made the access-model change.

Nicole’s judgment was that this changed the role of the product.

Old question:

> Is Typekit worth buying?

New question:

> Does this make Creative Cloud more valuable to keep?

The unit of value had changed.

## Ownership

> My GM made the access-model change.

> **I saw that it changed the role of the product, and I led the repositioning and GTM work that followed.**

## Positioning method

Actual working questions:
- What changed?
- For whom?
- What proof would make the promise credible?

Do not add a generic Method footer.

## Consequence

Canonical headline:

> **If the product had a new job, everything around it had to change too.**

The position affected:
- Product + experience
- Customers
- Partners
- Market + Sales
- Measurement

Canonical sentence:

> **This wasn’t only a messaging change. The new position changed what we named the product, how customers found it inside Creative Cloud, how legacy users moved over, what partner attribution looked like, and what we chose to measure.**

## Audience translation

Canonical heading:

> **The position stayed the same. The proof changed by audience.**

Creative Cloud members:

> Your Creative Cloud plan now includes the complete Adobe Fonts library, with no sync limits.

Legacy Typekit customers:

> Everything you loved about Typekit is still here. What changed are the limits.

Foundry partners:

> More reach through Creative Cloud, without disappearing behind the Adobe brand.

Sales:

> If you’re paying for Creative Cloud, you’re paying for fonts.

Do not imply that every line is a verbatim archival quote.

## Reality check

Partner concern: Adobe was putting its name on a service built from independent foundries’ work. Some partners feared disappearing behind the Adobe brand.

Canonical response:

> **I kept the strategy. I changed the plan.**

What stayed:
- the product needed to become Adobe Fonts

What changed:
- foundry identity and contribution needed more visibility in the story and experience

Supporting learning:

> Feedback can improve execution without invalidating the strategy.

## Proof

Canonical proof framing:

> **Usage told us the launch worked. Retention tested whether the position was right.**

Approved proof:
- +40% MAU in the first year
- 0 foundry partner losses
- causal retention analysis across about 3.25M Creative Cloud Complete subscribers
- more than 40% lower next-month churn among Adobe Fonts adopters
- analysis repeated annually
- retention evidence became part of the executive case for continued investment

Canonical transition:

> **We had positioned Fonts as a reason to keep Creative Cloud. So a year later, I commissioned the analysis that could actually test that claim.**

Canonical proof close:

> **We measured what the position promised. Fonts had become a reason members stayed.**

## Final theory

> **Positioning starts before the story. It starts with deciding what job the product plays in the business.**

Do not add a metric recap, Method footer, Demonstrates footer, or competency summary after the conclusion.

# Editorial guardrails

Visitor-facing copy should be direct, specific, plainspoken, thoughtful, experienced, unsentimental, and sayable aloud.

Avoid consultant language, AI-generic prose, jargon, stacked abstractions, and em dashes.

Avoid words and phrases such as leverage, unlock, ecosystem, operating system, strategic thesis, stakeholder contract, holistic, align, and north star.

# Ownership guardrails

Never inflate Nicole’s ownership.

Distinguish clearly between Nicole’s judgment, executive decisions, Engineering ownership, Finance ownership, Legal ownership, formal contract ownership, and implementation by product teams.

# Proof guardrails

Proof should test the claim the case makes.

Do not append unrelated impressive metrics.

Do not add metric recaps after conclusions.

Do not turn qualitative observations into formal KPIs.

# Source-of-truth rule

Use:
- repo root for context
- docs for decisions
- final approved case implementations for visitor-facing case content and internal case design
- current integrated implementation as reference, not authority, when it conflicts with approved docs or final case implementations

After integration, no older visitor-facing case copy should remain reachable in the live app.
